News Reading for Gen Z

UI/UX Research & Design · May 2026

I designed a news-reading service for Gen Z readers who find full articles daunting. It aims to make articles easier to approach and support sustained reading.

Question

Why do people who are interested in the news still struggle to read full articles?

Research

Review of news services, user interviews, think-aloud sessions, and grouping of recurring themes

Insight

The main barriers were long sentences, unfamiliar terms, missing context, and dense information, rather than a simple lack of reading ability.

Design

Interest-based discovery, context previews, plain-language explanations, in-article definitions, adjustable detail, audio, and rewards

01 / Entry

Offer context before readers open an article

The home and search screens introduce topics and key context before users open an article, making a full story feel less daunting.

02 / Reading support

Support readers as they work through the full article

I explored brief summaries, plain-language explanations, definitions, paragraph breaks, and audio on the article screen so readers could choose the support they needed.

03 / Participation & reward

Give readers a reason to return after finishing an article

A conversational quiz lets readers check what they understood, while points offer an incentive to come back for another article.

04 / Prototype evaluation

How did readers respond to the different forms of support?

Eight adults in their twenties read the same business article in its original format and in seven versions with different reading supports, thinking aloud as they went. This comparison summarizes what helped them and what challenges each approach introduced.

A format that feels easier to read does not necessarily preserve context or inspire trust. The findings point toward in-article explanations, selectable levels of detail, and clear paths back to the original text and its source.

This qualitative evaluation used one business article. The responses do not rank the prototypes or measure improvements in comprehension or reading speed. The service's audio, quiz, and rewards were not part of this comparison.

1News Reading for Gen Z

Abstract

This UI/UX research and service design project addresses the difficulty many Gen Z readers face when reading full news articles. Existing news services make it easy to discover breaking stories, but often provide less support for understanding an article once it is open.

The project focuses on helping readers start and keep reading the original article. The proposed journey offers context before reading, explanations during reading, and a simple way to reflect and continue afterward.

Problem

Many news services are designed for quick discovery and updates, with less attention to the experience of reading the full article. Long sentences, dense paragraphs, unfamiliar terms, and limited background knowledge can make a story feel demanding.

Even after an intriguing headline draws someone in, difficulty grasping the context or repeated interruptions in understanding can push them toward summaries or short videos. The issue lies in the barriers built into the reading experience, rather than a lack of interest in the news.

Research Process

I moved from problem framing and reviews of news services to user interviews, think-aloud sessions, and grouping of recurring themes. The service review compared article previews, summaries, glossaries, audio, and focus modes as ways to reduce reading effort.

In interviews and think-aloud sessions, I examined how people choose articles, what they expect before reading, where they stop, and how they feel about full-length news. I grouped recurring observations into four barriers: difficulty starting, missing context, unfamiliar terms, and difficulty continuing.

Insight

Participants often anticipated fatigue before they even began reading. Uncertainty about whether they could understand the whole article made it harder to start; unfamiliar terms and unbroken paragraphs made it harder to continue.

The design response was to build layers of support around the original article. Readers need context before they start, timely help as they read, and a gentle way to engage with what they have read afterward.

Design Application

I organized the service around discovery, understanding, and continued engagement. Interest-based categories and article cards make stories approachable, while a three-line preview provides context before the full article.

Within the article, plain-language explanations, definitions, paragraph breaks, audio, and adjustable levels of detail help readers engage with the full story. A conversational quiz and points extend the experience after reading.

Outcome

The final concept connects article discovery, reading support, and post-reading engagement in one service. It aims to help people keep reading full stories, not simply consume shorter versions of them.

This project explores how information structure and cognitive effort shape sustained reading. Observing where readers struggled informed both the service journey and the support offered within the article.

2To-do mate

Abstract

This UX redesign addresses the gap between making a plan with AI and acting on it. People may plan a routine with a tool like ChatGPT, only to enter the same information again in a scheduling app. That extra work can weaken their motivation to follow through.

I integrated AI recommendations into To-do mate's existing context so a plan could become an actionable routine without starting over. The goal was to help users get started while leaving the final decisions and adjustments in their hands.

Background & Problem

Many scheduling tools focus on recording and checking off tasks, leaving planning separate from execution. Re-entering an AI-generated plan in another app interrupts the path from intention to action.

To-do mate already had an AI assistance feature, but it did not clearly explain when to use it or what it would do. Users could find the feature without understanding how it fit their task.

Insight

Research and the comparison test suggested that users wanted AI to make getting started easier, rather than decide their schedule for them. They appreciated suggestions but wanted to retain control over the final setup.

The conversational flow also felt easier to follow and more personable than a sequence of separate setup screens. How AI was woven into the experience mattered more than simply having an AI feature.

Benchmark Review

A review of related services showed that recommendations often left users to repeat detailed setup steps. When suggestion, entry, and confirmation were disconnected, the help could feel like another task to complete.

That observation became a design principle: a recommendation should lead directly to something the user can put into practice.

Solution Design

The redesign makes the AI entry point clearer and connects planning to routine creation. Users can review a suggested schedule and quickly adjust the days, times, and reminders before saving it as their own.

I designed two approaches. Option A made each setup step explicit; Option B allowed recommendations and adjustments to unfold within a conversation. The latter offered a smoother path through the task.

A/B Test Results & Interpretation

In a test with eight participants, Option B scored higher than Option A on ease of use, usefulness, emotional connection, and overall satisfaction. Participants said the chat flow felt more responsive to their circumstances and less tedious to configure.

Option A made the steps clear, but asking users to confirm each one added effort. The comparison suggests that the continuity between a recommendation and the user's adjustments is central to this AI experience.

Takeaway

This project shows how a scheduling tool can connect planning with action. AI can reduce friction before a task begins while keeping users in control of what they ultimately commit to.

The To-do mate concept brings generative AI into routine planning without handing over the final decision. It offers one direction for designing productivity tools around both convenience and user agency.

Hear. Breathe. Heal.

Service Design · Plant-inspired stress relief app · October 2025

I designed a plant-inspired stress relief service that pairs mood check-ins and sound recommendations with a growing plant, making everyday self-care visible.

Problem

Why are emotional wellness apps easy to start but difficult to keep using?

Research

Review of stress relief services, user journey analysis, and feature design

Insight

A plant metaphor offered a gentler way for users to notice change than direct analysis of their feelings.

Design

Mood check-ins, personalized sound suggestions, plant growth, a plant collection, and a daily care routine

01 / Mood check-in

Check in through a few simple questions

Short questions help users reflect on their mood and stress during the day. Multiple-choice prompts make checking in feel less demanding than a formal assessment.

02 / Sound recommendation

Suggest sounds that match the user's mood

The check-in leads to a mood chart and sound recommendations. Users can choose a sound that feels right for them and take a moment to rest.

03 / Plant growth & collection

Make repeated moments of care visible through plant growth

Daily visits and listening sessions help a virtual plant grow. Its progress and the plant collection give users a visual record of care that might otherwise be hard to notice.

3Hear. Breathe. Heal.

Abstract

This service design project uses plants to support stress relief and emotional well-being. Hear. Breathe. Heal. invites users to notice how they feel, pause, and return to a gentle care routine over time.

Analyzing or documenting emotions can itself feel demanding. I designed simpler ways for users to check in and notice small changes without making self-care feel like another task.

Problem

Many meditation and stress relief services can be hard to use consistently. Logging feelings or checking in may feel like extra work, while change is rarely immediate or easy to see.

Overly clinical or direct language can also create distance in an emotional wellness service. The experience needed to let users notice their feelings at their own pace and return without pressure.

Insight

The research suggested that a visual metaphor could help users reflect on change without having to explain or analyze every feeling. A growing plant became a gentle way to represent that progress.

The central insight was that small, repeatable moments of care could encourage regular use more effectively than forceful prompts. Users needed to feel they were caring for themselves, rather than being managed.

Service Structure

The service has three parts: a mood check-in, sound recommendations, and plant growth. After answering a few questions, users can listen to a suggested sound. Returning to the app and listening over time help the plant grow, making their routine visible.

Mood records accumulate through brief interactions instead of lengthy journal entries. The growing plant and collection help users see how often they have returned to care for themselves.

Design Application

The design centers on a plant metaphor. I shaped the screens and tone so checking in and listening each day would feel like a calm care routine, avoiding language that sounds overly diagnostic.

Plant growth, a collection, and daily visits show progress as a pattern of small changes rather than a score. This gives users a reason to return without adding emotional pressure.

Outcome

The final concept is a plant-inspired stress relief app for checking in, finding a suitable sound, and seeing a record of small acts of care. It uses everyday interactions and visual change to make emotional reflection feel gentler.

This project explores how an interface can support continued use without pressuring users to feel better on a schedule.

A Cup of Pause Tea

Package Design & Illustration · March 2025

I designed a tea packaging experience that turns a short break into a moment of rest, using restrained botanical illustrations and materials chosen for a natural, understated feel.

Problem

How can tea packaging communicate both product information and a sense of rest?

Research

Brand concept, botanical illustration, packaging structure, and information hierarchy

Insight

Drinking tea can offer a moment to pause, beyond simply consuming a product.

Design

Botanical illustrations, recycled-paper textures, a restrained palette, and generous white space

01 / Package overview

Packaging that makes room for a pause

Brand cards, package mockups, dielines, and labels work together to present product information while evoking a moment of rest.

4A Cup of Pause Tea

Abstract

This tea packaging project began with the idea of taking a brief pause in a busy day. I treated tea as an invitation to slow down and translated that idea into understated botanical illustrations and a calm packaging design.

Packaging holds the product, but it is also a customer's first encounter with the brand. Alongside ingredients and product details, I wanted it to convey a sense of rest from the first glance.

Problem

Tea can create a moment of pause, yet packaging often concentrates on flavor, ingredients, and benefits. That functional emphasis can leave little room for the feeling of rest at the start of the experience.

The project therefore considers the whole interaction of picking up, looking at, and opening the package, so useful information and a calming impression work together.

Insight

People may experience tea as a brief change of pace as well as a drink. The packaging should explain the product while establishing a quiet, reassuring mood from the first encounter.

Packaging can set expectations for the brand experience before the tea is brewed; it is more than a surface for product information.

Design Approach

"Pause" became the guiding idea. Sparse botanical illustrations, muted colors, and generous white space invite the viewer to slow down. I relied on the balance between imagery and empty space instead of added decoration.

Product details are easy to scan, drawing the eye from the feeling created by the front panel to the practical information on the back. A recycled-paper texture reinforces the brand's material character.

Implementation

Botanical illustrations and the product name appear on the front; ingredients and descriptions are arranged clearly on the back and sides. The visual system continues when the package is unfolded.

I carried the same visual language across brand cards, labels, box dielines, and tea tins. I balanced the amount of information with white space so the package felt calm and easy to explore.

Outcome

The final design extends the act of drinking tea into an everyday pause. It balances practical product details with a sense of calm from the moment someone picks up the package.

louis poulsen

Website Redesign · May 2025

A website redesign that brings the brand's identity into focus

Problem

How can visitors experience the brand's history and character as they explore its products?

Research

Website analysis, restructuring brand and designer content, and redesigning key pages

Insight

When the brand story and product information are disconnected, visitors may encounter a catalog before they understand the atmosphere and thinking behind the lighting.

Design

Stronger brand storytelling, reorganized designer content, clearer product discovery, and redesigned key pages

01 / Website overview

A website redesign that brings the brand's identity into focus

I designed landing, About, designer, product listing, and product detail pages to carry the brand story through the browsing journey.

5louis poulsen

Abstract

This project redesigns the website of lighting brand louis poulsen. I examined the site structure and how it presented the brand, then reorganized the pages to make the brand's identity clearer as visitors explore products.

The brand is about more than the shape of its lamps: the quality of light, the designers' ideas, and its history also matter. The redesign connects that story to the experience of exploring its products.

Problem

On the original site, brand storytelling and product browsing felt separate. Visitors could reach product listings before getting a sense of the atmosphere and ideas behind the lighting. Those connections were not carried clearly across pages.

For someone new to the brand, this separation could make it harder to understand what distinguishes louis poulsen. Browsing for information and experiencing the brand felt like two separate tasks.

Design Direction

I aimed to strengthen the brand story while making product discovery more coherent. The landing, About, and designer pages foreground the history and design approach; listing and detail pages make products easier to compare and explore.

The guiding principle was to connect learning about the brand with choosing a product as parts of one journey.

Page Structure

The redesign covers the landing page, About page, designer profiles, product listings, and product details. Large imagery and concise copy establish the mood on the landing page, while the About and designer pages pair visuals with the brand's history and design philosophy.

Product cards and information are arranged to make shapes and colors easier to compare. Detail pages connect photographs, descriptions, and related content, supporting both efficient browsing and a richer sense of the brand.

Visual Strategy

Visually, I kept the brand's understated elegance and the atmosphere created by its lighting. Generous space, large images, and a clear type hierarchy reduce clutter and let product and interior photography carry the mood.

A consistent tone and rhythm across pages help the experience feel continuous even as visitors move between learning about the brand and comparing products.

Outcome

The result is a website concept that expresses the brand more clearly while simplifying product discovery. Visitors can learn about its history and ideas, then move naturally into the collection without losing that context.

This project explores how information architecture and visual design work together on a brand website. It shows the value of making product browsing efficient without losing the character that makes the brand distinct.

WHO ATE MY APPLE?

Book Design & Research · August 2025

A research-led book design that uses a detective story to make UX dark patterns easier to understand.

Question

Why can a manipulative interface feel like a choice we made ourselves?

Research

Research into dark patterns, classification of techniques, case analysis, and narrative editorial design

Insight

Dark patterns often work through familiar copy, defaults, hidden information, and the structure of choices on everyday screens.

Design

A detective story about a missing apple, case files, bold contrast, and analytical spreads

01 / Book overview

A book that investigates dark patterns through a detective story

I used the mystery of a missing apple to make dark patterns engaging and easier to follow. The book runs to 62 pages.

6WHO ATE MY APPLE?

Abstract

WHO ATE MY APPLE? turns my research on UX dark patterns into a detective story. A detective investigates who ate the apple, giving readers a narrative route through interface choices that steer or distort user decisions.

Rather than simply listing types of dark patterns, the book invites readers to investigate each case. The clues prompt them to look more closely at how a screen steers a decision. The book also helped shape my later research on marketing consent interfaces.

Problem

People may feel they are choosing freely online, but button placement, wording, defaults, hidden information, repeated prompts, and cancellation flows can all shape a decision. Dark patterns often hide inside familiar designs, making them hard to recognize.

These techniques may emphasize one action, shame someone for declining, or frame the available options unevenly. The options may still be visible even as the design pushes people toward one of them.

Research Focus

I collected examples of algorithmic recommendations, psychographic targeting, forced account connections, hidden subscriptions, and misleading delivery copy. I then examined how each interface shaped the choices available to users.

For each case, I compared what users might expect with the behavior the interface encouraged, paying attention to what it concealed or emphasized. This framed dark patterns as a problem of choice architecture as well as visual presentation.

Storyline

The book follows the question, "Who ate my apple?" The apple represents the reader's ability to choose, while the detective guides readers through the clues. Moving through the case files helps readers trace how interfaces can change a decision.

The mystery makes an abstract UX concept more approachable. Each case moves from an incident and its clues to an analysis, allowing readers to become invested in the story before uncovering the design mechanism behind it.

Content Structure

The book moves from an introduction and opening investigation to key clues, case files, analysis, and a conclusion. Each case begins as a small mystery; the following spread identifies the dark pattern at work and its implications for users.

Cases cover psychographic targeting, algorithmic recommendations, subtle pressure tactics, hidden subscriptions, and misleading delivery copy. Together they show how similar ways of steering choices recur across platforms.

Design Approach

I styled the book as a detective's case file. Strong color contrast, strikethroughs, large typography, page numbers, and clue labels give it the rhythm of an investigation.

I gave body text, case descriptions, analysis, and graphics distinct places in the visual hierarchy so the information would remain clear. I adjusted the density and emphasis on each spread to keep the cases engaging and legible.

Outcome

The finished book recasts dark pattern research as a detective story. Following the mystery helps readers notice how familiar interfaces structure and steer choices.

It brings research, writing, and editorial design together to make a complex UX issue more approachable. The project became a starting point for my later study of marketing consent interfaces.

01

Exploring Interface Design Considerations for Supporting the Reading of Full News Articles on Mobile Devices

Accepted for publication · September 2026 · Ji In Lee and Bo Ram Lee

Technical terms, limited background knowledge, and dense information can make full news articles difficult to follow on a phone. This study explores how reading support can ease those barriers while preserving the article's context and readers' trust in the news.

Research Question

How can mobile interfaces help people read full news articles without losing the context or credibility of the original?

Method

Eight adults in their twenties evaluated an original article and seven versions with reading support. We analyzed think-aloud sessions and interviews, guided by the Framework Method.

Key Findings

In-context definitions and a choice of detail levels helped participants navigate the article. Heavy simplification and strong visual treatment sometimes raised concerns about missing context or reduced trust.

Design Implications

Provide explanations within the article, connect summaries and answers to the original text, break content at meaningful points, and use visual cues with restraint.

02

Dark Patterns in Marketing Consent Interfaces and the Experience of Choice

Marketing consent screens often allow users to decline without losing access to a service. This study examines whether interface design can still shape perceived autonomy, awareness of manipulation, alignment between choice and intention, negative emotions, reactance, and consent decisions in that setting.

Research Context

Much of the literature on dark patterns examines privacy choices, such as cookie banners, that can affect access to a service. By focusing on marketing consent that users can freely decline, this study asks how design shapes judgment even when a real choice remains available.

Type Development

I reviewed 60 popular iOS apps and identified recurring patterns in marketing consent flows. The resulting six categories cover benefit framing and confirmshaming, visual hierarchy, preselection and hidden information, repeated interference, social proof, and urgency or scarcity cues.

Experiment Design

I created seven mobile prototypes: one neutral screen and six dark pattern conditions. The planned repeated-measures experiment will ask participants to evaluate every condition and report their likely choice, perceived autonomy and manipulation, alignment with their intentions, negative feelings, and reactance.

Analysis & Contribution

The planned analysis compares each dark pattern with the neutral condition and examines differences across the six patterns. The study aims to show why consent rates alone may not capture how visual hierarchy, defaults, information visibility, emotional copy, and repeated requests affect the experience of choosing.

03

How Need for Cognition Shapes Responses to Marketing Consent Interfaces

Drawing on the Elaboration Likelihood Model and need for cognition, this ongoing study examines whether combinations of design elements in marketing consent interfaces affect people differently. The same screen may be interpreted differently depending on how much a user prefers to engage with information.

Theoretical Frame

The study treats marketing consent as a setting in which users retain a meaningful choice. It draws on the model's central and peripheral routes to examine whether people who enjoy effortful thinking attend more to the reasoning behind a request, while others respond more to layout and visual emphasis.

Design Factors

An analysis of 60 apps identified four design dimensions: visual, verbal, structural, and choice-related. Two levels of each dimension produce a 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 design, represented in 16 Figma prototypes.

Experiment Design

The planned survey experiment will recruit 160 participants. Each person will evaluate four randomly assigned interfaces to limit fatigue. Need for cognition will be measured with the six-item NCS-6 scale.

Analysis & Contribution

The planned analysis uses mixed-effects models to account for repeated responses and individual differences. By examining autonomy, usability, and behavioral intentions, the study aims to explain how combinations of design features interact with users' information-processing preferences.
